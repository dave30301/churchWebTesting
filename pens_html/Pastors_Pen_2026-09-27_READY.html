The Rhythm of the Redeemed

Sunday, September 27, 2026  |  Music and Arts Sunday
 

A Word From Your Pastor

Church, I am still carrying this morning with me.

This was Music and Arts Sunday, and it was also Communion Sunday, and I do not think that was an accident. We spent the morning talking about what happens when a redeemed people find their rhythm, and then we came to the table to celebrate the One who gave it to us.

If you were in the sanctuary or with us on the livestream, thank you for giving God your whole self. If you missed it, I want you to have it anyway. That is what this letter is for.

Sunday Recap

I started where the archives of human history start. Look back over everything we have been through as a people and you will find that the human spirit has a divinely inspired power that refuses to stay quiet when God is worthy to be praised. Genesis says we are made in the image of God, and part of carrying His image is that when God has been good to us, we have a way of voicing it. It is innate. The trouble is that we have learned to suppress it. Sometimes we would rather be cute than be holy.

So I took you back to the antebellum South, beneath the weeping willows and the Spanish moss. After days of being worked and beaten on the plantation, our ancestors would slip away to the hush harbors down near the water, where the enslaver could not hear them. Around a campfire, with their faces glowing, a man or a woman would step into the middle of that little circle and start to hum. Soon the other voices joined in rhythmic harmony, and the agony of the day turned into a defiant declaration of faith. Hear me carefully: they did not sing to survive. They sang to remind themselves that though their bodies belonged to an evil enslaver, their souls still belonged to God. The plantation controlled the bodies. It never controlled the songs in their souls.

They had no organ, no keyboard, no grand piano and no microphone. What they had was a rhythm from heaven. They stomped their feet in the dirt and it became a drumbeat. They clapped their hands and it became the bassline. And when things got so heavy they could not find the words, they hummed and they moaned, and God knew how to translate their pain into praise. In whispers and in spirituals like Go Down, Moses and Didn't My Lord Deliver Daniel, they were teaching us our theology, and here it is: in the biblical narrative, music and dance are never just entertainment. They are acts of holy defiance, acts of spiritual warfare and acts of embodied resistance.

From there I gave you three praise points. The first is the theological score, the protocol of praise. Psalm 150 is the finale of the hymnal of Israel. The book opens in Psalm 1 with a meditative moment, and it closes with an unapologetic orchestration of praise, as if to say that in case you missed it across the first one hundred forty nine, here is what you need to know: God is worthy to be praised. And notice that praise the Lord carries an exclamation point. It is a command, not a suggestion. Then it tells you how, instrument by instrument. The Hebrew word we translate timbrel simply means tambourine, and the dance it calls for is physical, joyous whirling and twirling. That is not the devil. That is the Bible. And James closes the door on our excuses: "Do what God's word says. Don't merely listen to it, or you'll fool yourself."

I showed you that protocol in action in Exodus 15. After ten plagues Pharaoh finally let the Hebrew people go, God split the sea, they crossed on dry ground, and the oppressive system that chased them was swallowed up behind them. Then the text says Miriam the prophetess took the timbrel in her hand, and all the women went out after her with timbrels and with dances, and she answered them and said, "Sing to the Lord, For He has triumphed gloriously! The horse and its rider He has thrown into the sea!" Now do the math with me. Moses was eighty when God called him, Aaron was two or three years older, and Miriam was the oldest of the three, so she was in her upper eighties if not close to ninety. She had spent her entire life under Egyptian bondage. Her body knew the muscle memory of oppression. And what she decided that day was that her body would no longer hold the shape of her bondage. Church, because you belong to God, your body does not have to hold that shape either. Music and dance break the muscle memory of the trauma this world tried to leave in you.

The second point is the contemporary track, arts, activism and the church. The Black church has always been the cultural and spiritual incubator where faith and the arts meet, and our music has never been isolated from our struggle. It was music that fueled the civil rights movement. They marched and they voted singing Ain't Gonna Let Nobody Turn Me Around and Keep On Walking, Keep On Talking. Today our young people are facing new Pharaohs, and we are watching anxiety, hopelessness, economic disinvestment and systemic racism do their work. So hear me: if the world can provide the soundtrack for your pain, the church must provide the symphony for your purpose and your praise.

Music and arts ministry is not a Sunday performance. It is a communication strategy and a community engagement. When we teach a child, a teenager or a senior to give their voice to God, to play an instrument, to write poetry, to paint, to mime or to dance, we are telling them their voice and their talent have purpose in God. Back when I worked at Capital One I had a habit of humming a gospel tune on my way in, softer when times were good and louder when times were hard, and I found out it did not only encourage me. We have to take the melody out of the sanctuary and carry it into the streets, because right now the streets are hearing everything but God.

The third point is the communion cadence, the Master's composition. Paul told the church at Ephesus that we are God's workmanship, and the Greek word there is the root of our English word poem. You are God's poem. You are His masterpiece, His song, the symphony of praise He is still writing so the world can know who He is. Humanity took the harmony of God's creation and turned it into a song of rebellion and brokenness, and we went out of tune with heaven. But the great conductor did not throw away the sheet music. Two thousand years ago God came down in Jesus, the hummed melody of His grace, and the world tried to silence the song of heaven. They nailed His hands, thinking that if they could still the hands they could stop the clap and the bassline. He was silent Friday night. He was silent Saturday. But on Sunday morning the angelic praise team showed up with a new song, and Jesus stepped out of the grave and rewrote the soundtrack of human history. That is why your past does not have the final word, and that is why we do not come to the communion table for a funeral. We come for a victory celebration.

Life Applicable Gems

Here is what I want you to carry with you into this week.

1. Praise is a command, not a suggestion.

Look again at how Psalm 150 opens. Praise the Lord. That is an exclamation point, not a period and not a comma. The Psalm does not ask you whether you feel like it. It tells you to do it, and then it tells you how: with the trumpet, with the lute and harp, with the timbrel and dance, with strings and flutes, with loud cymbals and clashing cymbals.

2. An extraordinary God deserves an expressive response.

You cannot celebrate an extraordinary God with an indifferent praise. He woke you up. He brought you through last week, last month and last year. He is still making a way. Give Him something that matches who He has been to you.

3. Hearing is not obeying.

James said it plainly: "Do what God's word says. Don't merely listen to it, or you'll fool yourself." Some of us know the command and leave the praising to somebody else. That is not relationship. That is fooling yourself.

4. Your body does not have to hold the shape of your bondage.

Miriam was somewhere in her upper eighties, maybe ninety, and she had spent her whole life under Egyptian oppression. Her body knew the muscle memory of it. But when God delivered her she picked up the tambourine and she danced anyway. Because you belong to God, do not let your voice, your hands or your feet keep the muscle memory of what God has already brought you out of.

5. Praise is holy defiance.

In the biblical narrative, music and dance are never just entertainment. They are acts of holy defiance, acts of spiritual warfare and acts of embodied resistance. Our ancestors in the hush harbors knew it. Sing anyway, and you have told the enemy he does not get the last word.

6. The church owes the world a symphony.

If the world can provide the soundtrack for your pain, then the church must provide the symphony for your purpose and your praise. Somebody is already writing music for the hurt. We have to take the melody out of the sanctuary and carry it into the streets, the schools, the corporations and yes, into Congress.

7. You are God's poem.

When Paul wrote that we are God's workmanship, the Greek word he used is the root of our English word poem. You are God's poem. You are His masterpiece and His song, and He is still writing so the world can hear who He is through your life.

Sit With These This Week

Read Exodus 15:20-21 and all of Psalm 150 in the New King James Version, and read them out loud if you can. Some scripture will not open up to you until you hear your own voice say it. Then ask yourself one question: where in my life have I been sitting on a praise that God already paid for?

Let everything that has breath praise the Lord. Praise the Lord!

Psalm 150:6, NKJV

From My Desk To Yours

One more word before I let you go. If this week is heavy, do not wait until you feel like praising to praise. Miriam lifted the tambourine because of what God had already done, not because of how she felt. Let your praise go first and let your feelings catch up to it.

And if life feels out of tune today, I want you to know that Jesus is inviting you to His choir. If you are carrying something you have not told anybody about, come and see me. That is what I am here for.

Your Servant and Pastor,

Rev. Dr. Keith Savage
